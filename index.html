    What the data set needs to look like

    The worker view depends on six things existing in Snowflake. Four of them are source data. Two of them, the action log and the consent registry, usually do not exist yet and are the reason most projects like this stall.

    The pipeline in Snowflake

    1. RawEach source lands unchanged in its own schema with load time stamps.
    2. IdentityA master person index links records across systems to one person_key.
    3. ConformedOne event table and one daily snapshot per person, shared vocabulary.
    4. ModelScores each eligible action for each person and writes recommendations.
    5. AppReads secure views filtered by consent and by the worker's role.

    The two tables that usually do not exist

    Action log. A model can only learn which step works if someone recorded which step was taken. Every recommendation, every action a worker took, who took it, and how it ended has to be captured as structured data. Case notes will not do. This is the single biggest gap in most communities.

    Consent registry. One row per person, per data domain, per recipient organization, with effective and expiry dates. Snowflake row access and masking policies read this table so that withheld data never reaches the screen or the model features for that person.

    A third table makes the best contact feature possible: a contact attempt log recording which staff member tried to reach the person, where, when, and whether it worked.

    What the model needs from that data

    • A training table at person period grain. Each row is one person at one decision point, with features known as of that date, the action taken next, and the outcome observed later. Features must never include information recorded after the decision date.
    • Defined outcomes. Housed within 90 days, still housed at 6 and 12 months, and return to homelessness. These come from HMIS and must be agreed before modeling begins.
    • Variation in actions. If every person with a given profile always received the same step, the data cannot say whether a different step would have worked better. Recommending a step is a causal question, so the estimates need propensity adjustment or uplift methods, and ideally some staged rollout.
    • Reliable linkage. Names, birth dates, and partial Social Security numbers differ across systems. Linkage error rates should be measured and carried as match_confidence, since a wrong link puts one person's history on another person's screen.
    • Missing data treated as information. No emergency visits can mean good health or no access. The absence of a behavioral health record can mean no need or no consent. The snapshot must distinguish unknown, withheld, and none.
    • Fairness checks. Justice and health data carry the bias of the systems that produced them. Recommendations and error rates should be compared across race, gender, age, and disability groups before any worker sees them.
    • Explanations with every score. Each recommendation should store its top contributing factors, such as SHAP values, so the worker sees why and can disagree.

    A realistic order of work

    • Start with HMIS and coordinated entry, which the Continuum of Care already governs, plus the action log and consent registry.
    • Ship a rules based next step first. It is transparent, useful on day one, and it generates the action and outcome history a model needs.
    • Add one outside domain at a time under a signed data use agreement, beginning with whichever partner is most willing.
    • Train and validate the predictive model once roughly a year of logged actions and outcomes exists, and keep the worker in charge of the decision.
